How St. George's Carly Knudsen Got Recruited to Snow College

Category: College Recruiting

Tags: College Soccer Recruiting, Snow College, St. George Utah, Youth Soccer Development

If your child wants to play college soccer, start earlier than you might think. Look at schools and email coaches in the sophomore year of high school. Go to ID camps the summer before junior year. Train at home every day, especially first touch, off-ball movement, strength, and running, and keep grades high, because coaches look at that too.

That advice comes from Carly Knudsen, a Desert Hills High School captain from St. George who now starts at forward for Snow College. We talked with her about college soccer recruiting, youth soccer player development, and what she would do differently if she had it to do over again.

How do kids get recruited for college soccer?

College soccer recruiting is rarely just about being seen at one showcase. Play at a high level with your club, then be proactive: introduce yourself to coaches, follow up by email, and make it easy for them to remember you. Travel tournaments help, but a personal introduction and a timely email can open a door that waiting around will not.

Carly traveled with her club teams, including trips to California for high-level tournaments. What led to Snow College was more direct. She went to a Snow versus Utah Tech game, walked up to the coach afterward, introduced herself, and followed up by email.

"I decided to go to a game that Snow played against Utah Tech at. And I just went up to the coach afterwards, and I just introduced myself." Carly said she "emailed him afterwards." And "he was interested."

He had a spot, and she took it. Teach your child how to walk up, say their name, and send a clear follow-up. That is often how kids get recruited for college soccer.

When should my child start contacting college coaches?

Start looking at schools and emailing coaches in the sophomore year of high school. Then, the summer before junior year, go to ID camps, introduce yourself in person, and keep your name in front of coaches, including on Instagram. Waiting until senior year is later than most families think.

That is the change Carly would make if she went through recruiting again.

"If I went through it all again, I would start literally, like, my sophomore year of high school, start looking at schools I wanted to go to and start emailing." She went on, "And then by the time I'm a junior, I go to a bunch of ID camps the summer before my junior year and just introduce myself to coaches, make sure I get my name out there, post on Instagram."

If your player is a freshman or sophomore, build a short list and draft a simple email now. If they are already a junior or senior, start anyway.

What should my child train at home?

The work that travels with your child is simple and daily: technical footwork, strength, and running. Carly found that thirty minutes of solo footwork a day sharpened her first touch and made her more confident on the ball. In high school, add weight training so she doesn't get pushed off the ball.

"I did footwork, technical footwork all the time on my own. And I weight lifted on my own. And I did running on my own to stay in shape." She said, "I found that when I did footwork ... on my own for thirty minutes, a day, it helped my touch be so much better. And I was able to perform better, and I was more confident on the ball."

Asked what parents should focus on right now, she pointed to first touch, off-ball movement, grades, and strength: "I would tell them to get a very good first touch and to be good off the ball movement and to keep their grades up because grades are so important, and coaches look at that." She added, "I would tell them to ... start weight training once you're in high school. So that you aren't getting pushed off the ball."

Do grades matter for college soccer recruiting?

Yes. College coaches look at grades, and the habits that keep school on track often show up on the field too. Keep a simple daily schedule, track homework and due dates, and treat class with the same seriousness as training. Strong grades also keep more programs within reach.

Carly was named to the NJCAA All-Academic First Team as a freshman and is majoring in Exercise Science, with plans to become a sports physiologist. "I keep track of my classes and my homework, and I make schedules of my day. I plan out my day." She also connected school to the field: "if you're performing in class, you'll be able to perform on the field."

What's the jump to college soccer like?

The biggest jump is speed and intensity. The game is faster, decisions have to come quicker, and players need to run more and be in better shape. Even standout club or high school players will find that everybody at the college level is good, and everybody works hard to get there.

"The college level is a lot more fast paced." It is also "a lot more intense." Carly said, "The speed of play is a lot faster. You have to make decisions quicker, and you have to be able to run faster and be in better shape for college."

The harder adjustment is realizing you are no longer the exception. In her words, "you could be, like, the best person on your club team or your high school team, but then once you get to college, everybody is good, and everybody puts in a lot of work to get to the place that they are."

What if my child isn't getting much playing time?

It is common, and it is hard. Many players go from being a starter to earning minutes all over again. Stay mentally strong, control what you can control, keep a good attitude at practice, and keep doing the extra work. Playing time often follows that consistency.

Carly lived this at Snow, and she had seen it in high school too. "I wasn't playing as much at first. And it definitely was, like, more of a mental battle." There were games she "didn't even touch the field."

She kept working anyway. "I just kept doing my footwork in the mornings, and going to practice with a good attitude." She focused on what was in her control so she could "give myself an edge" and support her team. She said that "if you keep on giving your best and working hard, then eventually, you'll get more playing time and adapt to the level of play that college soccer is."

This season, as a sophomore, she has been starting and "playing almost full minutes ... every game." As a freshman in 2025 she appeared in 14 games with one start, and still contributed three goals and three assists.

About Carly Knudsen

Carly Knudsen is a sophomore forward (No. 20) for Snow College women's soccer and an Exercise Science major from St. George, Utah. At Desert Hills High School she was a four-year varsity player, a two-time 4A All-Region second-team selection, a 2024 4A All-State honorable mention, and a senior captain. She played club for Rebel Elite 06G Black (2021 to 2024) and Fire FC 06 ZH (2024 to 2025). As a freshman at Snow in 2025, she appeared in 14 games with one start, scored three goals, added three assists, and was named to the NJCAA All-Academic First Team. She plans to become a sports physiologist.

FAQ: College soccer recruiting

How do kids get recruited for college soccer?

Compete in strong club environments and high-level tournaments, then introduce yourself to coaches and follow up by email, the way Carly did after a Snow versus Utah Tech game.

What should my child train at home for youth soccer player development?

Daily technical footwork (Carly did thirty minutes a day on her own), running, and, once they are in high school, weight training, with a focus on first touch and movement off the ball.

Do grades matter in college soccer recruiting?

Yes. Carly said grades are "so important, and coaches look at that." A simple daily schedule for homework and training helps on the field and keeps more college options open.

What is the biggest difference between high school or club soccer and college soccer?

What should my child do if they are not playing much at first?

Treat it as a mental challenge, not a dead end. Keep a good attitude at practice, do extra technical work, and control what you can control. Carly went from limited minutes as a freshman to starting and playing nearly full minutes this season.
